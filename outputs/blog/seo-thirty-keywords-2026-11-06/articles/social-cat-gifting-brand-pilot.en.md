# Social Cat for Brands: Scope a Gifting Pilot

Status: local review draft; confirm the publication date before release.

Run a Social Cat product gifting pilot with separate checks for delivery, publication, creative acceptance and licensed use.

![An open sample carton holds a ceramic coffee dripper and paper packaging.](../public/assets/blog/social-cat-gifting-brand-pilot.jpg)

Social Cat connects brands with creators and describes gifted, affiliate and paid collaborations in its help center. For a gifting pilot, decide whether you are sending an unsolicited sample or offering a defined assignment the creator agrees to. Track receipt, submission, publication, acceptance and usage permission separately. A pilot without a cash creator fee still uses products, shipping, staff time and possible reshoots. Calculate cost against accepted outputs, and plan disclosure for endorsements under the rules that apply.

## Define the pilot around one question

Start with one question the pilot can answer. Suppose a coffee equipment company needs to know whether a first-time buyer can see where the paper filter goes in a vertical demonstration. Use the same model and invite willing creators to deliver a short demonstration. Have a reviewer check visibility, components and the named model. The [Social Cat gifting page](https://thesocialcat.com/influencer-gifting-platform) describes setting product details, target audience and content requirements. Use that workflow as vendor documentation and keep demo figures out of the forecast. Use [a useful creator brief](https://www.quickkol.com/blog/write-a-useful-campaign-brief/#answer) to name the main task, reviewer and date. If the aim is to collect viewer questions from a public release, specify the channel, observation window and coding rule separately. One main criterion helps the team avoid changing the standard after seeing the numbers. A short video can pass the production review without answering a separate distribution question. Decide whether the pilot primarily buys a reviewable demonstration, a public placement or both. An unsolicited parcel without accepted terms gives the brand an inventory and delivery record. A collaboration with an agreed brief gives the team an actual assignment to review.

## Record collaboration type and agreed output together

The [Social Cat collaboration types guide](https://help.thesocialcat.com/en/articles/3296066) distinguishes gifting, affiliate and paid work and says brands pay creators directly for paid and affiliate arrangements. A category is the beginning of the scope discussion. Record who accepted, who keeps the sample, any production fee, whether posting is required, which account is involved and which files are expected. A delivered parcel does not establish assent to make a video. Suppose a creator can try the product but cannot post publicly within the requested window. The brand can record a sample-only arrangement, agree to an asset delivery with its own rights, or close the invitation. Reconcile the change before allocating inventory. Track shortlisted, invited, accepted and canceled as separate statuses. A marketer who inherits the pilot should be able to tell which people are still deciding and which assignments actually have agreed dates. If a paid task replaces a gifted one, preserve both the earlier proposal and the accepted change so finance can reconcile it. Keep shipping and any fee responsibility visible in the updated brief.

## Start the production clock at confirmed receipt

Check the model, color, instructions and required accessories before shipment. Keep recipient information in the approved logistics system. In the project record, distinguish dispatch, carrier delivery, creator receipt and readiness to film. Suppose the carton arrives on time but the paper filters are missing. The creator may need the missing items to demonstrate the setup accurately. Record what was missing, when a replacement can arrive and whether the filming window moves. Treat fulfillment and creator deadlines as distinct when a product problem prevents the work. A creative reviewer needs the model and readiness status, not the private delivery address. The illustration shows what a receipt check can look like. In the timeline, confirm who can approve a substitute sample. A different version may change the claims, the filmed process or the relevance of footage already captured. If a package is marked delivered but the creator has not confirmed it, leave it in a pending state and use the normal support route. Update the expected date when the production constraints actually change.

## Review disclosure in the actual publication format

[FTC influencer disclosure guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers) treats free products as a possible material connection that should be understandable when viewers see the endorsement. For a U.S. audience, review where the disclosure appears in the actual video, image and copy alongside the platform requirements. Check the relevant obligations for other markets. If an editor cuts the opening from a longer video, verify whether the material relationship is still clear in the short version. Ask creators to describe their actual experience and relevant limitations. Brand review can correct a product identifier or inaccurate instruction while preserving the creator’s honest view. Keep the approval and the published version together. If you cannot confirm what appeared in a placement, hold reuse for that version until the question is resolved. A customer who sees only the clip may not see the original profile or longer caption. Give the editor the evidence and conditions they need to keep the version intelligible. Put the disclosure check next to the actual asset in the acceptance record.

## Accept publication, files and licenses separately

List the exact outputs, such as a native post, downloadable edit, clean master or raw footage. For each, check media, territory, term, editing, paid distribution and music. The [Social Cat gifting page](https://thesocialcat.com/influencer-gifting-platform) describes licensed content as a use case. Verify the actual campaign terms for the asset before relying on that description. Use the [creator usage rights guide](https://www.quickkol.com/blog/creator-rates-usage-rights/#answer) to distinguish file delivery from permission. Suppose a native post passes review while advertising rights remain unresolved. Accept the publication in its own row and keep advertising pending. If the brand wants a crop, translated overlay or new market, check the permitted edit and territory. Tie each version to an approval record so the operator can stop or renew its use on time. A creator’s organic post might use audio that the brand cannot reuse in its own media. Have the rights reviewer inspect the actual planned version rather than the brief alone. A screenshot of an existing post can establish how it appeared on a date. It cannot settle ownership of the raw material or the scope of a later license.

## Use accepted outputs in a hypothetical cost example

In a hypothetical pilot, eight units cost 18 dollars each, packing and postage cost seven per unit, and six staff hours are estimated internally at 30 dollars per hour. Recorded cost totals 380 dollars. Five accepted videos give a cost of 76 dollars per accepted video. Three accepted videos would give about 126.67 dollars at the same recorded cost. List any platform fee, tax, licensing fee and replacement shipping that the calculation leaves out. If no deliverables pass review, leave unit cost undefined and show the expense. The calculation describes the pilot’s measured effort and output. It does not estimate return or establish a platform average. If the brand needs ad-ready footage, include in that denominator only accepted edits with the corresponding paid-use permission, and keep the native post count separately. Suppose five clips pass creative review but only two are licensed for ads. Report five accepted clips and two ad-ready assets, with the chosen cost method explained. Do not silently treat the original shipment count as the number of outputs delivered.

## Find the constraint before expanding

At closeout, compare accepted invitations, receipt, timely submission, creative acceptance, publication and clearance. Identify the first material gap. A rejected clip may use the wrong model or hide the action, while a late submission may reflect delivery or coordination. For published content, read permitted results and relevant questions at comparable post ages. Give site visits and orders their own source and window. With only a few participants, use the observations to revise the next brief or fulfillment step. The pilot does not establish the performance of the entire marketplace. Give the next owner a route to the actual asset and approval. Suppose the video receives more views than planned but still cannot show the filter. The media result can stay in the report while the production team changes the shot. If the next pilot is intended to test whether a closer camera angle helps, keep the exact version, publishing window and question classification visible. The next round should improve an identified constraint and establish which remaining question still needs evidence.

## Preserve the original record when timing or scope changes

Keep delays, cancellations and scope changes with the original assignment instead of dropping failed rows. If a creator cannot film soon after delivery and both sides agree to move the date, retain the original due date, reason and new date. If the brand changes the product at the same time, give the new model its own footage and approval record. Review the new submission against a clear criterion and keep the products, rights and windows distinct. Preserve the aggregate evidence needed for the closeout and assign any unresolved item. For example, a warehouse problem might require a replacement while a later editing request affects only the brand’s own cut. Give each owner the action they can verify. The next budget review can then count costs that genuinely arose in the pilot and estimate only changes that are supported by the record. The same file should make it clear which assignment ended and which is still open. Before repeating the pilot, verify the actual effort involved in replacements, returns, inventory and licensing review. Share the constraints that matter with the next group of participants. If the team changes the sample or deliverable count, give the revised project a new scope and keep the earlier cost basis visible.
