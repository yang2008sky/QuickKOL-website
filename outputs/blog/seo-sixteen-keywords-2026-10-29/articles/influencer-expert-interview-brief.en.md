# Working With an Influencer: Write an Expert Interview Brief

Define the audience question, expert experience, evidence requests and factual review process for a useful brand interview.

When interviewing an expert influencer, start with the audience decision and choose someone with relevant experience who can explain their evidence and limitations. The brief should specify questions, permitted evidence, confidential material, factual review and editing rules. A brand can correct product facts; paying for an interview does not create a right to make the expert endorse every conclusion.

## Connect the interview to a specific audience decision

A request to discuss industry trends often produces an answer that could accompany almost any brand. Replace it with a decision: what should a small business confirm before its first software migration? How can a repair shop judge whether a tool demonstration represents everyday work? These questions invite explanations of choices and tell the editor which passages deserve space.

Describe what the audience already knows, the constraints it faces and the action it should be able to take afterward. A newcomer learning terminology needs different examples from a procurement team that already has a technical proposal. If the interview serves several audiences, choose a main version and treat other needs as extracts or follow-up assignments. One short video cannot carry every educational and sales task.

List the brand’s desired messages separately from the audience’s actual questions, then look for a useful intersection. The [executable campaign brief guide](/blog/write-a-useful-campaign-brief/#answer) helps organize these inputs. A strong interview brief allows the expert to explain where a feature does not fit. Understanding a boundary can help a reader make the next decision more than another broad recommendation. Use the audience problem to judge an answer’s usefulness before judging how often the product name appears.

## Research relevant experience rather than relying on a title

Expertise needs to match the question. Someone who has operated a shop may explain scheduling and inventory without being qualified to certify technology for an entire industry. Look for public work that repeatedly addresses comparable tasks. Can the person distinguish direct practice, other people’s research and personal judgment? A persuasive biography is not a substitute for work relevant to this assignment.

Retain links, dates and specific reasons from a few public examples. Explains a complex workflow in usable steps is more informative than sounds professional. If the assignment depends on a current qualification or professional license, verify it through an appropriate first-party route and record the scope. Do not infer credentials from audience size, clothing or a workplace backdrop.

The [personalized creator outreach guide](/blog/personalized-creator-outreach/#answer) helps turn research into an invitation. Identify the work you reviewed, the question you want to discuss and its connection to the person’s experience. An invitation should not preselect the answer. Ask the creator to flag questions beyond their experience and any competing partnership, employer restriction or other condition that must be resolved first. This can reveal the need to change the subject or choose another interviewee before either party commits production time.

## Build questions that reveal reasoning

Move from the task’s context to an actual choice, then ask about evidence, failure conditions and the reader’s next step. How do you decide the team is ready, followed by which missing input would make you delay, is more likely to produce a reusable method than a request for three benefits. Leave room for follow-up rather than turning the interview into a spoken advertising script.

Give each main question an editorial purpose: obtain a definition, comparison, demonstration or explicit limitation. When an answer drifts, the host can return to that purpose instead of chasing a keyword. Share topics and likely evidence needs beforehand. Unless precision is necessary for the material, do not require every natural answer to be finalized in advance.

Avoid combining unrelated questions or embedding a conclusion the guest is expected to accept. Asking when they would choose another approach reveals a solution’s boundaries. If an unexpected number appears, mark it for a later evidence request rather than immediately promoting it into a subtitle. A clear interview structure should expose reasoning as well as conclusions. It should also let the host stop a line of discussion when the necessary evidence is absent.

## Separate experience, claims and verifiable facts

I use this in my own work and every business can cut costs in half with it are different kinds of statements. Personal experience still needs to be represented honestly; the broader claim needs support matching its scope. The [FTC endorsement FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking) discusses endorsements and material relationships in US advertising. An expert’s voice is not a route for publishing advertising claims the brand could not support itself. Check the requirements relevant to the actual market.

Label important sentences in the working transcript as direct experience, editorial opinion, product fact, external statistic or hypothetical example. Product teams supply version-specific facts, outside statistics retain dates and sources, and illustrative scenarios remain explicitly hypothetical. Professional standing does not automatically verify every statement. A general disclaimer cannot repair an overly certain conclusion in the main explanation.

Limit evidence requests to information that changes the conclusion. Do not demand unrelated client lists or private business records. An anonymized example, public material or process explanation without confidential numbers may work instead. If essential evidence cannot be provided, narrow or remove the claim. The interview can remain valuable without implying that the team has independently verified a result for every reader or every possible operating condition.

## Agree on factual review and editorial boundaries

Agree on who reviews what, when feedback is due and how disagreements are resolved. The brand checks product names, features and public documentation. The guest checks their experience, wording and attributed views. The editor owns context, length and presentation. Anyone can flag an error, but factual review should not become an unlimited opportunity to renegotiate the interview’s conclusions.

Distinguish correcting an error, restoring necessary context and changing a position. An incorrect product version needs correction. Removing a limitation the guest expressed may change the meaning and requires discussion. If review produces a reshoot, rerecording or substantial re-edit, document changes to scope, fee and schedule instead of assuming unlimited revisions were included in the original quote.

Maintain a time-coded review copy with a decision and reason beside each material comment. If an attribution is disputed, hold the passage and find an accurate formulation. Silence should not be treated as approval of every edit. Agreeing early on how to handle disagreements reduces a last-minute dispute over control of the speaker’s voice. It also gives the producer a practical basis for distinguishing a correctable detail from a change requiring a new agreement.

## Preserve conditions when creating short extracts

A long interview often becomes short videos, sales training clips or quotation graphics. Review each version for its subject, conditions and visible limitations. Changing worked for our small team into works for teams may already alter the claim. A stronger promotional moment does not justify deleting the premise that made the answer accurate.

The [U.S. Copyright Office overview](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/) provides background on original expression and rights. The actual project should separately specify recording, editing, captions, translation, advertising, third-party distribution and term. An interview fee does not replace a clear permissions record. Check the allowed use of client footage, demonstration materials and other people’s work rather than assuming anything visible on the guest’s screen is available for reuse.

Keep the original time code, approved version, purpose and expiry arrangement for each extract. Review translations for names, technical terms, numbers and strength of language, particularly conditions such as not necessarily or in this situation. If the main interview is updated, the index helps locate older extracts still circulating. There is no need to produce a version for every platform. Start with formats that actually serve the intended audience and can preserve the meaning.

## Include the relationship in the published version

Identify whether the brand paid for the interview, supplied a sample, covered travel or has another material relationship. Confirm disclosure for the channel and market rather than leaving it inside the internal brief. [YouTube branded-content guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/154235?hl=en) requires appropriate notification of relevant paid promotions and describes responsibilities beyond the platform setting. A toggle does not resolve every presentation issue.

Inspect the actual publication: the opening, description, short extracts and translated versions should communicate the relationship appropriately. A clip may circulate independently, leaving the original video’s explanation behind. Make disclosure part of version review alongside captions, product facts and permissions instead of adding an almost unreadable sentence at the end of production.

Do not package an independent expert’s experience as comprehensive certification of the brand. Explain in the invitation and brief whether the fee buys discussion, production or publication, and which judgments remain the guest’s own. Clear treatment of the relationship helps the audience interpret the content. It also prevents a later sales asset from attributing a position or commitment that the interviewee never made. Recheck this when an educational interview is repurposed into a more explicitly promotional placement.

## Rehearse the brief with one concrete example

Suppose a business software company invites an operations adviser to discuss a first data migration. The audience is small businesses without a dedicated IT team, and the content job is explaining preparation. Three main questions might concern data ownership, field cleanup and a trial run, rather than asking the guest to declare the software best for every company. This is a hypothetical planning scenario, not a QuickKOL client case.

During rehearsal, the host asks what happens when a dataset has no owner. The adviser may recommend resolving responsibility before demonstrating an import. That answer helps the audience and reveals a prerequisite for the product demonstration. The brand can show the feature accurately in another segment; editing should not make the expert appear to have verified every outcome.

Finally, check whether the editor can locate evidence, decide which passages are usable and create an extract that preserves its conditions. If the team still has to guess who approves, where the clip may appear or what the fee includes, complete the record first. A useful interview handoff lets another colleague explain why the content is credible, whom it serves and what must be checked before its next reuse.

## Interview brief decisions

| Item | Specify | Handoff evidence |

| --- | --- | --- |

| Audience task | Specific question to understand or decide | Main question and editorial purpose |

| Factual review | Review scope, timing and disputes | Time-coded comments and decisions |

| Extract reuse | Context, channels, term and translation | Approved version and source index |

| Commercial relationship | Fee, support and appropriate disclosure | Review of actual published version |

## Before recording

Replace production-day guesses with recorded decisions.

- [ ] Retain relevant work and experience checks.

- [ ] Give main questions an editorial purpose and evidence need.

- [ ] Review relationship disclosure in each actual version.
